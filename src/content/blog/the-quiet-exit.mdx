The first exit was loud. Term sheets, lawyers, a dinner where someone made a toast I can't remember. It felt like the end of something.

The second one was quieter. By then I knew what the morning after looks like.

What nobody tells you about a successful exit is the weight of the pause that follows. The thing you built is no longer yours. The people who worked alongside you are being quietly re-orged into a structure that wasn't designed for them. The clock you'd been running against for years suddenly has no hands. And you – the one who pushed through every fire, every close call, every board meeting where the runway was shorter than the roadmap – you are supposed to be celebrating.

## The identity problem

Founders don't talk enough about the identity problem on the other side of an exit. For years, the company *is* you. You answer the question "what do you do?" with its name, not yours. You wake up thinking about its problems. Friends know your moods by the color of the pipeline that week.

Then it ends, and you're still you, but without the scaffolding. That's not a crisis. It's an opening. But it takes a while to recognize which one.

## What I wish someone had said

If I could go back, I'd tell myself three things.

First, the money doesn't solve the identity problem, and pretending it does extends it. Buy yourself time to think, not time to consume.

Second, the instinct to jump into the next thing is a trauma response. Sit with it for a beat. Write down what actually gave you energy versus what just made you important. Those are different lists.

Third, the relationships you built during the build are more durable than the ones you built because of the exit. Protect the first. Be careful with the second.

## Why this is on my website

Because a lot of what I now do – advising founders, sitting with CEOs in the hard seasons – started here. In the space between exits. In the realization that the playbook for building and the playbook for transitioning aren't the same document and can't be written by the same person at the same time.

If you're in that space right now, reading this on a Sunday night, I see you. It's not a failure of strategy. It's the part nobody writes about.

–S.
